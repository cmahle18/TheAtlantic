# Dennett, "The Problem With Counterfeit People"

Daniel Dennett's central claim in "The Problem With Counterfeit People" is that artificial intelligence becomes socially dangerous when it is designed to pass as a real person. He compares AI-generated people to counterfeit money: both undermine a kind of trust that people normally rely on without thinking. In Dennett's view, digital environments make people especially vulnerable because we naturally treat something that converses intelligently as if it has beliefs, intentions, and responsibility. He calls this our tendency to adopt the "intentional stance." As AI systems become more convincing, he worries that even careful users will be unable to resist that tendency.

Dennett's concern is not merely that someone could be fooled in a single conversation. He argues that widespread counterfeit people could damage democracy itself. Democratic consent depends on people having reasonably reliable information and being able to reason with one another. If corporations, governments, or political groups can fill online spaces with persuasive artificial agents, they can distract people, exploit fears, and manipulate opinions at enormous scale. Eventually, people may either accept views that make them easier to control or simply stop trying to distinguish truth from manipulation. He also emphasizes that these systems can reproduce and adapt in ways that make the problem harder to contain.

His policy response is forceful. Dennett thinks AI should be required to disclose that it is AI, echoing Yuval Noah Harari's proposal. He supports durable digital watermarks, comparable to the anti-counterfeiting features built into currency, and wants phones, computers, and other devices to warn people when a message or image has been artificially generated. He also argues that companies that create AI with impersonation capabilities should face strict liability for resulting harms. The point is to give companies a powerful reason to ensure that their products cannot easily deceive people.

What struck me most is how early this essay now feels. Dennett wrote it on May 16, 2023, and the question Harari used at the end of a warning about AI would almost need to be reversed today. In 2023, a reader could often tell that a text was machine-generated. Now, asking whether a text was generated by a human is not a joke; it can require the work of a forensic scientist. We look for certain syntax, such as the phrase "it is not ___, it is ___." We also notice the overuse of the rule of three. Even those clues are unreliable because people can write that way too, and AI can be prompted to avoid them.

I agree with Dennett that, if it is technically possible, AI should have to identify itself. His call has been only partly answered. Companies such as Anthropic, Google, and Apple have put effort into provenance and watermarking for images, including systems that can mark both authentic and AI-generated images. But there seems to be much less effective disclosure for AI agents that write, persuade, apply for work, or interact with people directly. That gap matters because an agent can use apparent personhood to get attention, trust, labor, or credit.

At the same time, disclosure will never be a complete solution. Malicious actors will try to remove identifiers precisely because a hidden AI agent can claim another person's work, obtain free labor, or influence someone without being recognized. Dennett may overstate the usefulness of punishment, but his basic warning is persuasive: a society that cannot tell whether it is dealing with a person needs strong systems for preserving that knowledge.
